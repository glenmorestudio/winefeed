English estates launch prestige cuvées rivalling Champagne at £100-plus

  • Nyetimber released England's first prestige cuvée in 2018, a white and rosé priced at £150 and £175, positioning itself against premium Champagne under the '1086' label.
  • Five estates now produce super-premium sparkling wines at £100 or above: Nyetimber, Chapel Down, Gusbourne, Langham and Squerryes, each with distinct positioning and vintage strategies.
  • Extended lees ageing is a defining marker, with producers holding wines for seven to eight years minimum to develop structure and balance for the prestige tier.
  • Langham's Tommy Grimshaw pioneered a perpetual solera-style cuvée using a single Slavonian oak foudre, blending multiple vintages and releasing 650 bottles in June 2025 at £109.95.
  • Squerryes launched its prestige cuvée Patience 2014 in July 2026, building on the family's 300-year heritage as wine merchants and 20 years of estate winemaking investment.

Italian food group Compagnia del Gusto acquires UK wine merchant Jascots

  • Compagnia del Gusto Holding, founded by the Cosulich family in 2025, has acquired Jascots Wine Merchants from Freixenet Copestick through its UK subsidiary CDGH UK.
  • The Italian group operates three divisions: Compagnia del Mare (seafood), Compagnia delle Vigne (wine), and Compagnia dei Sapori (regional foods); it targets €200 million turnover within five years.
  • Jascots, founded in 1986 and rescued from administration by Freixenet Copestick in December 2020 after Covid lockdowns, will now distribute Compagnia del Gusto's Italian wine portfolio to UK on-trade customers.
  • Jascots retains existing relationships with Bolney Wine Estate, Champagne Alfred Gratien, and Schloss Johannisberg; Omar Raafat continues as head of the merchant.
  • Ettore Nicoletto, CEO of Compagnia del Gusto Holding, cited the UK as strategic for premium Italian wines and Jascots as a proven on-trade distribution platform.

Anishka Jelicich named CEO of New Zealand Winegrowers

  • Anishka Jelicich, a New Zealand native and former global director of responsible marketing at Pernod Ricard in Paris, has taken over as CEO of New Zealand Winegrowers, the industry body.
  • She replaces Philip Gregan, who led the organisation for 24 years and retired in June after witnessing New Zealand wine exports grow from near-zero recognition to NZ$2.1 billion annually.
  • New Zealand wine is now the country's eighth-largest export, with wines shipped to more than 100 markets and 288.772 million litres exported in 2025.
  • Jelicich said her first priority is to listen to industry stakeholders before setting strategic direction, citing her global perspective gained from two decades working in international wine and spirits.
  • NZW chair Fabian Yukich praised her strategic perspective and ability to build alignment across stakeholders as the sector navigates future opportunities and challenges.

US wine industry lacks unified marketing body to counter health messaging

  • Gino Colangelo, founder of New York PR agency Colangelo & Partners, says US wine lacks the coordinated marketing infrastructure that dairy, almonds and cranberries maintain through dedicated collective bodies.
  • Come Over October, a pro-bono campaign co-founded by Colangelo and wine critic Karen McNeil, has reached 3 billion media impressions and 5.6 million social impressions since its 2020 launch countering anti-alcohol narratives.
  • Wine regions that sustain consistent marketing, Wines of Georgia, Chianti Classico, Prosecco DOC Consortium, Chile, build market share through incremental campaigns, while sporadic efforts fail to maintain traction.
  • Colangelo says funding and industry support for advocacy campaigns arrive inconsistently, and warns that without sustained investment, the current US wine downturn will deepen and lengthen.
  • Competition from THC beverages, GLP-1 weight-loss drugs and sober-curious trends among younger consumers add pressure on an industry that cannot match the coordinated marketing of other agricultural sectors.
0530 JUL 2026

Australian wine exports fall to 22-year low as global consumption drops

  • Wine Australia reported exports fell 7% in value to A$2.3bn and 6% in volume to 598 million litres in the year to June 2026, the lowest volume since 2004.
  • Global wine consumption has fallen to its lowest level since 1961, driven by cost-of-living pressures, changing consumer habits, and competition from alternative beverages.
  • Australia's three largest export markets all declined: mainland China fell 15% to A$756m, while UK and US shipments hit 25-year lows despite Australia remaining the top imported wine supplier to China.
  • UK weakness centred on lower and mid-priced wines, while premium bottles proved resilient; US demand softened across most price categories due to lower retailer stock and stronger domestic competition.
  • Canada bucked the trend with exports up 20% in value to A$188m, partly from US wine shortages during trade disputes, while premium Australian wines surged in Thailand and Singapore.

Bordeaux wildfires force tourist evacuation, threatening wine region's only growth sector

  • Wildfires in Gironde region near Bordeaux have forced 220,000 evacuations and destroyed 240 homes, prompting French government to urge tourists away during peak summer.
  • Wine tourism was Bordeaux's fastest-growing revenue stream; Edouard Le Grix de la Salle, winemaker, said the crisis cuts off the sector's momentum as other wine sales decline.
  • Château Picque Caillou's director Peggy Plouhinec temporarily closed due to smoke and warned hospitality earnings will suffer from fewer tourists this summer.
  • Most vines escaped direct flame damage; Thomas Duroux, director general of Château Palmer in Margaux, said 2025 harvest quality is not threatened by fire itself.
  • Smoke taint risk remains uncertain: vineyards within 50-100 km downwind of fires warrant monitoring, though 2022 fires caused only isolated issues in Graves parcels adjacent to flames.

RNDC files Chapter 11 after shedding most US wine distribution markets

  • Republic National Distributing Company, once the second-largest US wine and spirits distributor, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in Texas yesterday to manage an orderly shutdown of remaining operations.
  • RNDC lists liabilities between $1 billion and $10 billion against assets of $500 million to $1 billion, with over 100,000 creditors and $300 million in top unsecured claims led by $93.92 million owed to Proximo Spirits.
  • The company sold off most US markets in recent months to competitors including Reyes Beverage Group, Columbia Distributing, and BreakThru Beverage Group, claiming preservation of 5,000 jobs in the transition.
  • Small wine and spirits suppliers face severe risk; many lack leverage to demand upfront payment and depend on RNDC as their only route to market, potentially losing hundreds of millions in unpaid claims.
  • Roughly 1,450 workers remain employed; RNDC sought court approval to continue paying wages and benefits to retain staff with knowledge of supplier and customer relationships during the shutdown process.

WHO classifies alcohol as carcinogen; NHS maintains moderate drinking guidance

  • WHO classifies alcohol as a Group 1 carcinogen alongside asbestos and tobacco, citing toxicity and dependence risk.
  • UK NHS advises both men and women not to exceed 14 units weekly, roughly ten small glasses of lower-strength wine.
  • A 175ml glass of 12% ABV wine contains up to 158 calories; low-alcohol alternatives often compensate with residual sugar.
  • Laura Catena, winemaker and medical doctor, argues peer-reviewed studies show moderate drinkers have lower all-cause mortality and urges individual consultation with doctors.
  • Debate persists between public health bodies: WHO emphasizes carcinogenic risk while NHS permits moderation, reflecting tension between abstinence and pleasure in wine culture.

Denmark Hill station grows first railway vineyard in Britain

  • Denmark Hill railway station in south London will release its first wine next year from 30 Bacchus vines planted on station property by the Camberwell Society.
  • Winemakers Nick Mair and Tony Coleman hand-cleared and terraced the slope without mechanised tools to establish the vineyard, which they describe as a very small-scale affair.

  • Bacchus, the variety planted, is England's fourth most popular grape and thrived last year, yielding 9.5 tonnes per hectare against its five-year average of 6.75 t/ha.

Heat shifts European drinkers toward cold wines, away from red

  • During Europe's June heatwave, French on-trade wine sales fell 25% while water jumped 36% and soft drinks rose 18%, suggesting extreme heat drives drinkers away from alcohol entirely.
  • Fine wine buyers are already shifting: iDealwine's prior survey found 45% seek more white wine as temperatures rise, 74% favour older vintages, and only 18% actively hunt lower-alcohol options.
  • Producers responded by planting rosé from red vineyards, promoting blanc de noirs whites from red grapes, and rebranding lighter styles: Chile launched Chile Pacífico for crisp whites and Pinot Noir, while Bordeaux revived 'claret' for cold-served reds.
  • Burgundy whites like Chenin Blanc and Riesling, plus lighter reds based on Cabernet Franc and Grenache, are expected to gain ground as hotter summers persist, according to iDealwine co-founder Angélique de Lencquesaing.
  • Research from UC Davis, ETH Zurich and North Carolina State University found alcohol sales rise with temperature only up to 32°C, then decline as people stay indoors during extreme heat.

Ancient vine-training systems vanish as labor costs and mechanization reshape European vineyards

  • Italy's alberata system, which trained vines on poplar and elm trees across Naples until the late 20th century, has shrunk to a handful of hectares, replaced by mechanizable methods like Guyot.
  • Lanzarote's hoyos, volcanic-ash pits built after 1730s eruptions, draw thousands of tourists annually and support the island's economy, yet vineyard area fell 19 percent between 2000 and 2026 due to extreme labor demands.
  • Spain's Rías Baixas region maintains pergola training for Albariño because specialized machinery reduced labor intensity and the DO bans mechanized harvesting regardless of system, making tradition economically viable.
  • Veneto's bellussera hybrid, which once supported silk production and subsistence farming, fell from common use to 9 percent of Piave DOC vineyards, but climate resilience and wine tourism may reverse its decline.
  • The loss of historic training systems erodes local economies, cultural heritage, and viticultural diversity needed for climate adaptation, forcing regions to negotiate between heritage preservation and economic survival.

Brooklyn public housing complex paves pathways with 450,000 recycled wine corks

  • The Public Housing Community Fund, in partnership with Cork Collective and local nonprofits, opened recycled cork pathways and playgrounds at Marlboro Houses in Gravesend, Brooklyn as part of a $500,000 renovation.
  • Cork Collective, launched in 2024 by architecture firm Rockwell Group and cork manufacturer Amorim, sourced over 450,000 wine bottle corks from New York City restaurants and elsewhere to create the installation.
  • The corks were ground and mixed with adhesive at a Wisconsin recycling center before being laid; cork surfaces absorb stormwater, reduce ambient temperature by 20 to 30 percent, and avoid plastic or asphalt toxins.
  • Less than one percent of the 13 billion cork stoppers manufactured annually are recycled; three billion are discarded in the United States each year.
  • Children from the Marlboro Houses community submitted design mockups that shaped the final layout of the playspaces and pathways.

Vineyards test netting to cool fruit zones and preserve berry quality

  • UC Davis found shaded Cabernet Sauvignon clusters at Oakville Experimental Vineyard retained 27 percent more anthocyanins than unshaded fruit during heat events, as temperatures above 35°C degrade color compounds.
  • Luis Reginato, head of viticulture at Catena Zapata in Mendoza, uses light netting to create alternating pulses of direct and filtered sunlight rather than continuous exposure, reducing vine stress in high-altitude desert conditions.
  • Marco Simonit's 2025 trials at Petra in Suvereto, Italy, showed Merlot clusters under 90 percent shade nets cooled from 50°C to 40-41°C, dropping sunburn incidence from 41 percent to 3 percent in one block.
  • Achille Boroli at Boroli in Castiglione Falletto found an open net reduced maximum Villero temperatures from 49.2°C to 43°C, but darker nets exceeded 50°C, proving net density and color both determine cooling effectiveness.
  • Net placement matters: heated fabric touching grapes causes burn, and covering both sides of canopy rows risks trapping heat, requiring strategic deployment on sun-facing sides only.

Grape sunburn spreads north as heat waves reshape vineyard practices

  • Sunburn in grapes is a physiological stress triggered by berry surface temperatures exceeding 104-109°F under high light, causing chlorophyll and polyphenol breakdown that produces asphalt, leather, and creosote flavors in finished wine.
  • Chris Tanghe, MS, director of education for GuildSomm, identified sunburned grapes by their flattened fruit profile and notes of oxidized bitterness, rubber, and asphalt, a sensory signature now familiar to sommeliers tasting across warm regions.
  • Joanna Gambetta, Ph.D., wine scientist at University of Newcastle, Australia, found that early defoliation at flowering builds photoprotective compounds in berries, reducing sunburn damage compared to defoliation at veraison.
  • Producers in Provence and Napa Valley are reversing decades of practice: leaf removal in midsummer is now rare, white varieties are no longer stripped, and kaolin clay film has reduced canopy temperature by up to 9°C at Tyrrell's in Hunter Valley.
  • As heat waves arrive earlier and reach further north, avoiding direct sun exposure, through canopy management, reflective films, and harvest sorting, has become essential to preserve aromatic compounds and acidity in wine grapes.

Mourvèdre Struggles Despite Heat Tolerance as Warming Tests Bandol's Balance

  • Even Mourvèdre, a heat-loving grape that thrives in southern France's Bandol region, is losing acidity and balance as temperatures rise and droughts intensify, forcing winemakers to adapt.
  • Producers at Domaines Ott, Domaine Gueissard, and Domaine Tempier are experimenting with canopy management, higher pruning, shade trees, and deeper root systems to protect vines from sunburn and heat stress.
  • A three-year drought from 2022 to 2024 killed some old Monastrell vines in Spain's Jumilla region, where vineyard area has halved over 20 years as hotter southern sites become unviable.
  • Irrigation, long resisted as a threat to terroir identity, is becoming essential for survival in Bandol and Jumilla, though Bandol's appellation director says almost no growers have adopted it yet.
  • In Bandol, cooler sites traditionally reserved for rosé are now being used for red wine as producers shift to parcels likely to withstand climate change, potentially reducing the region's rosé output from its current 75 percent.
