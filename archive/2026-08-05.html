Endeavour Group axes 80% of Australian vineyards, profit crashes AU$374m

  • Sydney-based Endeavour Group, Australia's largest liquor retailer and winery owner, will sell most Australian vineyards and wineries to cut grape growing by 80 percent.
  • CEO Jayne Hrdlicka announced the group will shift to buying 99 percent of bulk wine and grapes from external growers instead of using estate fruit.
  • Profit forecast plummets from AU$426 million in 2025 to AU$52 million in 2026 after AU$311 million in asset writedowns and restructuring charges.
  • Endeavour is selling Chapel Hill in McLaren Vale, Oakridge in Yarra Valley, and Josef Chromy in Tasmania, but retaining Cape Mentelle and Dorrien Estate.
  • The divestment reflects broader Australian wine sector collapse: exports hit a 22-year low and the 2026 harvest fell 19 percent year-on-year.

UK fine wine distributors lose millions as consolidation looms inevitable

  • Jeremy Howard, CEO of Cru World Wine, says fine wine faces inevitable consolidation as top 15 UK companies collectively lose nearly £20 million yearly despite £850 million in annual sales.
  • The sector has too many small merchants with duplicated costs, masked during Covid demand surge but now unsustainable; top 15 firms spend £210 million on admin to sell £850 million in wine.
  • Brett Fleming of Armit Wines and Helen Miller of Goedhuis Waddesdon both expect consolidation to accelerate, citing inflation, fiscal policy headwinds, and pressure from restaurant closures like Heston Blumenthal's Dinner by Heston.
  • Howard argues smaller firms cannot afford modern technology and inventory management; a neutral technology platform owned by producers, merchants and collectors could reduce individual overheads and improve survival rates.
  • Industry leaders expect a 'leaner, more efficient world' but warn consolidation will likely eliminate administrative jobs, as the current model cannot sustain profitability across the fragmented distribution chain.

Bordeaux launches chilled, low-tannin Claret to reverse falling red wine sales

  • The Syndicat des AOC Bordeaux et Bordeaux Supérieur introduced Bordeaux Claret as an official 'mention traditionnelle' for the 2025 vintage, following a 2023 consultation on declining red wine drinking.
  • The style is a light, fruit-forward dry red made with short maceration, minimal tannin, and no oak, served chilled at 5°C, targeting younger consumers in the UK, France, Belgium, Germany, China and Japan.
  • The first vintage produced around 16,500 hectolitres, roughly two million bottles, from about 40 producers including major négociants Calvet and Mouton Cadet.
  • Winemakers must meet a defined sensory profile assessed by independent tasting panels, with minimum 11% ABV, maximum 7g/l residual sugar, and colour intensity above a specified threshold.
  • Bordeaux vineyard area has contracted sharply since 2021; the Claret category is positioned to reverse regional oversupply by appealing to by-the-glass service in restaurants and bars.

Ancient vine-training systems face extinction as labor costs and mechanization advance

  • Italy's alberata system, once widespread around Naples, survives on only a handful of hectares today, replaced by mechanizable methods like Guyot and vertical shoot positioning.
  • Lanzarote's hoyo system, carved into volcanic rock after 1730s eruptions, draws thousands of tourists annually and supports the island's economy, yet vineyard area fell 19 percent between 2000 and 2026 due to labor demands.
  • Alberto González Plasencia, owner of Bodegas Timanfaya on Lanzarote, warns that if hoyos disappear, tourists will stop visiting and local wine values will drop.
  • Spain's Rías Baixas region maintains pergola training for Albariño because specialized machinery reduces labor, and the DO bans mechanized harvesting across all systems anyway.
  • Bellussera, a 19th-century hybrid pergola system in Veneto, nearly extinct after the silk industry collapsed, may survive through climate adaptation and wine tourism.

Vineyards deploy shade netting to cool grape clusters during heat stress

  • Shade netting reduces cluster-zone temperature and protects anthocyanins, the pigments that degrade above 35°C; a 2022 UC Davis study on Cabernet Sauvignon found 27 percent more skin anthocyanins in shaded versus unshaded clusters.
  • Net color and placement matter critically: white and green nets perform better than black, which absorbs heat; nets must stay away from fruit to avoid transferring heat directly to berries, as Boroli in Barolo discovered.
  • At Petra in Tuscany, 90 percent shade nets dropped Merlot cluster temperatures from 50°C to 40-41°C, cutting sunburn incidence from 41 percent to three percent in one trial; partial shading preserves beneficial UV light.
  • Luis Reginato, head of viticulture at Catena Zapata in Mendoza, uses light netting (15 percent shade) primarily to reduce plant stress in dry conditions rather than limit radiation, since high-altitude desert vineyards receive excess sunlight.
  • Managing the fruit zone through netting is one strategy among many; row orientation, canopy design, water management, and site selection all shape how vines balance heat protection with phenolic development and wine quality.
0213 JUL 2026

Grape sunburn spreads as heat waves reach cooler regions; growers reverse leaf-removal practices

  • Sunburn occurs when berry surface temperature exceeds 104-109°F under high light; cells die, skins crack, and berries shrivel in severe cases.
  • Heat damage breaks down chlorophyll, polyphenols, carotenoids, and aromatic compounds, producing flavors of cooked fruit, asphalt, rubber, and creosote in finished wine.
  • Defoliation at veraison increases sunburn risk; defoliation at end of flowering allows berries to build photoprotective compounds, reducing damage significantly.
  • Producers including Chêne Bleu in Ventoux and Tyrrell's in Hunter Valley now avoid summer leaf removal on white varieties and limit it to early season or east-facing sides.
  • Kaolin clay spray cools canopy by up to 9 degrees Celsius; vineyard design, trellis orientation, and fruit-zone shading are now primary strategies.

Heat-Tolerant Mourvèdre Struggles as Southern France Temperatures Rise

  • Mourvèdre, long grown in Bandol and Jumilla for its heat tolerance, is now struggling to maintain balance as temperatures rise and droughts intensify, even in traditionally hot regions.
  • A three-year drought in Jumilla from 2022 to 2024 killed some old bush vines, forcing producers to consider irrigation despite its threat to terroir character.
  • Producers at Domaines Ott, Domaine Gueissard, and Domaine Tempier are experimenting with canopy management, higher pruning, and ground cover to shade vines and retain soil moisture.
  • Bandol's cooler sites, traditionally reserved for rosé production, are being converted to red wine as the region warms, potentially reducing rosé output from its current 75 percent of production.
  • In Jumilla, vineyard surface area has halved in 20 years as lower-altitude sites are abandoned; only higher-altitude parcels around 600 to 800 meters remain viable.
