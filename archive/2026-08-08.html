Ancient vine-training systems decline as labor costs and mechanization reshape European viticulture

  • Italy's alberata system, once common around Naples, survives on only a handful of hectares as Guyot and vertical shoot positioning replaced it over the late 20th century.
  • Lanzarote's hoyo pits, carved after 1730s volcanic eruptions, attract thousands of tourists yearly and sustain wine values, yet vineyards shrank 19 percent from 2000 to 2026 due to extreme labor demands.
  • Spain's Rías Baixas winegrowers maintain pergola emparrado training by adopting specialized machinery for maintenance; mandatory hand-harvesting rules mean labor intensity is uniform across all systems in the region.
  • Veneto's 19th-century bellussera hybrid, which once supported silk and subsistence farming, fell to nine percent of Piave DOC but may revive through climate resilience and wine tourism appeal.
  • Historical vine-training survival hinges on regional economics: tourism, local regulation, mechanization limits, and climate adaptation determine whether ancestral knowledge persists or vanishes.

Vineyards deploy netting to cool ripening fruit during heat stress

  • Vineyard netting, historically used for bird and hail protection, is now deployed to lower fruit-zone temperatures and preserve acidity, color, and phenolic balance during heat events.
  • A 2022 UC Davis study on Cabernet Sauvignon found shaded clusters had 27 percent greater skin anthocyanins than unshaded controls, as anthocyanins degrade above 35 degrees Celsius.
  • At Petra estate in Tuscany, Merlot clusters shaded with nets measured 40-41 degrees Celsius versus 50 degrees in unshaded blocks, reducing sunburn from 41 percent to three percent.
  • Luis Reginato, viticulture head at Catena Zapata in Mendoza, uses filtered light exposure via canopy management rather than full shade, letting light reach clusters in brief pulses.
  • Net color and placement matter: white and green nets perform better than black; nets must stay clear of clusters to prevent heat transfer, and covering both canopy sides risks trapping heat.
0313 JUL 2026

Grape sunburn shifts vineyard practices as heat waves spread

  • Grape sunburn is a physiological stress from excess light, UV, and berry surface heat that can exceed 122°F in 95°F air, degrading color, aroma, and acid.
  • Sunburned wine tastes of cooked fruit, asphalt, creosote, and rubber, with flabby acid, high alcohol, and premature aging that worsens over time.
  • Producers including Tyrrell's in Hunter Valley and Chêne Bleu in Ventoux have abandoned mid-summer defoliation and now shield fruit with late-season leaf cover or kaolin clay.
  • Early defoliation at flowering builds photoprotective compounds in berries; late defoliation at veraison leaves fruit exposed, so timing of canopy management is now critical.
  • Winemakers including Matthiasson in Napa and Tyrrell's use vineyard design, reflective sprays, and harvest-time sorting as last-resort defenses as heat waves arrive earlier and further north.

Heat-Tolerant Mourvèdre Struggles as Southern France Temperatures Soar

  • Mourvèdre, bred for heat and traditionally thriving in coastal Bandol and Spanish Jumilla, now faces stress even in its strongholds as summers intensify and drought persists.
  • A three-year drought in Jumilla from 2022 to 2024 killed some old bush vines despite their depth, forcing producers to consider irrigation for survival of the variety.
  • Bandol winemakers including Jean-François Ott of Domaines Ott and the Gueissard family are experimenting with canopy shading, higher pruning, and ground cover to protect grapes from sunburn and retain soil moisture.
  • Abandonment of southern vineyard sites in Jumilla has halved surface area over 20 years, with only higher-altitude parcels around 600 to 800 meters proving viable for heat-tolerant varieties.
  • Shifts in production are occurring: Bandol may reduce rosé output as cooler sites traditionally reserved for it are now needed for red wine, changing the region's wine style profile.

Global alcohol deaths fall 26% since 2010, analysis finds

  • Alcohol-attributable deaths worldwide fell 26% between 2010 and 2024, according to analysis by the International Alliance for Responsible Drinking of Global Burden of Disease data.
  • Age-standardised disability-adjusted life years linked to alcohol declined 23% over the same period, while high alcohol use exposure fell roughly 10%.
  • The International Alliance for Responsible Drinking attributes the trend to combined effects of changing drinking patterns, healthcare improvements, public health initiatives and broader development, not a single intervention.
  • The 26% mortality reduction exceeds the UN World Health Organisation's 2010 target of a 20% reduction in harmful alcohol use by 2030.
  • The International Alliance for Responsible Drinking cautioned that global averages mask regional variation and progress cannot be assumed to continue without sustained effort.
