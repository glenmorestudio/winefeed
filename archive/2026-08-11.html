Texas High Plains growers struggle as pandemic wine boom ends, contracts fade

  • Texas wine industry relies on 644-kilometre separation between High Plains growers and Hill Country wineries, a system built on handshake trust now under strain.
  • Pandemic surge from 2020 to early 2023 drove aggressive grape purchases and rising prices; when consumer sales softened, inventories backed up and buyers withdrew.
  • Growers now farm by contract tier: fully contracted blocks receive premium care; uncontracted fruit gets minimal attention to preserve cash, directly linking vineyard investment to buyer certainty.
  • March 2025 freeze lowered High Plains yields significantly, particularly whites, creating a painful reset that may rebalance supply but threatens growers already facing multi-year losses.
  • Major growers including Jet Wilmeth, Andy Timmons and Neal Newsom are retiring or reducing acreage, forcing wineries dependent on their fruit to confront succession risk and regional fragility.

  • Sancerre Sauvignon Blanc now sells for $40 to $50 per bottle, forcing buyers toward cheaper neighboring appellations: Pouilly-Fumé, Menetou-Salon, Quincy, Reuilly, and Coteaux du Giennois, most priced $20 to $30.

  • Paul-Henry Pellé, organic farmer at Domaine Pellé in Menetou-Salon, and Emile Balland in Coteaux du Giennois both cite acidity loss as the primary challenge from warming temperatures.
  • Nearly three-quarters of Sancerre production is exported, with half reaching the United States, while neighboring appellation exports remain much smaller despite rising quality and availability.

Soju surges past shōchū in U.S. market as Asian spirits gain share

  • Soju grew 133 percent between 2019 and 2025 to under one percent of U.S. spirits volume, while shōchū rose only 11 percent over the same period, according to IWSR consulting director Koryn Ternes.
  • Green bottle soju, the mass-produced sweet style made mandatory by Korea's 1965 rice ban, dominates U.S. exports and retail shelves, with Jinro the world's best-selling spirit for 25 consecutive years.
  • James Barlow, fine wine and Asia portfolio manager at Spec's in Texas, said the retailer purchased under $1 million in Asian spirits in 2018 but now buys multi-millions, crediting soju's fruity flavors and lower ABV.
  • Craft soju producers like Tokki Soju, founded in Brooklyn in 2016 by Douglas Park, are slowly gaining ground as consumers mature beyond green bottle brands and seek traditional expressions.
  • Shōchū brands iichiko and Mizu focus on bars and restaurants rather than retail, with higher-proof cocktail-ready options targeting bartenders as key ambassadors to reach whisky and mezcal drinkers.

RNDC files for Chapter 11 bankruptcy after shedding majority of markets

  • Republic National Distributing Company, formerly the second-largest US wine and spirits distributor, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in Texas yesterday to wind down remaining operations and seek buyers.
  • RNDC lists liabilities of $1-10 billion against assets of $500 million, $1 billion, with over 100,000 creditors and $300 million in unsecured claims led by $93.92 million owed to Proximo Spirits.
  • The distributor sold off majority of its markets in recent months to Reyes Beverage Group, Columbia Distributing, Martignetti Companies, and BreakThru Beverage Group after exiting California last June.
  • Bankruptcy does not include National Distributing Company or RNDC's six joint ventures in Illinois, Indiana, Kentucky, Michigan, Ohio, and New York, though the Alaska joint venture is included.
  • Small suppliers face potential losses as they lack leverage to demand payment upfront; approximately 1,450 remaining employees will continue to receive wages under a court-approved first day motion.

Ancient vine-training systems vanish as labor costs and mechanization reshape European viticulture

  • Italy's alberata system, once common around Naples, survives on only a handful of hectares after being replaced by mechanizable methods like Guyot and vertical shoot positioning.
  • Lanzarote's hoyo pits, dug after volcanic eruptions in 1730-1736, remain viable because tourism draws thousands of visitors; active vineyards fell 19 percent between 2000 and 2026 despite local subsidies.
  • Rías Baixas producers maintain the labor-intensive pergola for Albariño because specialized machinery now handles ground tasks, and the DO bans mechanized harvest anyway.
  • Veneto's bellussera hybrid, which once supported silk production and livestock, fell from common practice to nine percent of Piave DOC as those industries vanished; climate resilience and wine tourism may revive it.
  • Survival of traditional systems depends on region-specific economics: tourism, local regulation, mechanization aids, and climate adaptation determine whether heritage vineyards thrive or disappear.

Disease-resistant grape hybrids cut fungicide sprays 80 percent, gain European plantings

  • PIWIs, or fungus-resistant hybrid grapes, require two to three fungicide sprays per season versus eight to 15 for traditional varieties, reducing chemical, fuel, and labor costs.
  • Modern genetic testing technology rhAmpSeq, deployed since 2019 via the US VitisGen initiative, lets breeders identify disease-resistance genes with precision and stack multiple protective layers into single varieties.
  • German plantings reached 4,000 hectares in 2025, up 10 percent annually; France is developing ResDur hybrids crossed with Cabernet Franc and Petit Verdot to maintain regional signature while resisting mildew.
  • A 2026 Luxembourg study found PIWIs delivered twofold economic savings and threefold environmental benefit over traditional varieties, with largest gains in soil health and reduced water toxicity.
  • PIWIs require no change to existing vineyard practices beyond reduced spraying; industry success depends on tasting mature-vine commercial volumes and competitive retail pricing alongside sustainability messaging.

Vineyards deploy netting to cool fruit clusters amid heat stress

  • Vineyard netting, traditionally used for hail and bird protection, is now being engineered to reduce cluster temperature and preserve color, acidity, and phenolics during heat events.
  • UC Davis research on Cabernet Sauvignon showed shaded clusters retained 27 percent more anthocyanins than unshaded fruit when temperatures exceeded the 35-degree Celsius degradation threshold.
  • At Catena Zapata in Mendoza, light netting intercepts roughly 15 percent of radiation; Luis Reginato, head of viticulture, observes reduced vine stress in hot conditions without blocking beneficial UV exposure.
  • At Petra in Tuscany, Marco Simonit's 2025 trials found white and green shade nets more effective than black nets; Merlot sunburn dropped from 41 percent in unshaded control to three percent under 90 percent shade.
  • At Boroli in Barolo, netting placement matters critically: fabric must be kept away from clusters to avoid heat burn, and open nets outperformed dense ones, suggesting shade level and airflow balance the benefits against heat trapping.

Grape sunburn spreads beyond hot regions as heat waves shift vineyard practices

  • Sunburn occurs when berry surface temperature exceeds 104-109°F under high light, causing chlorophyll and polyphenols to degrade and producing flavors of asphalt, creosote, and leather.
  • Joanna Gambetta, Ph.D., wine scientist at University of Newcastle, Australia, found that defoliation at veraison increases sunburn risk; defoliation at end of flowering allows berries to build photoprotective compounds.
  • Producers in Provence and Napa Valley are abandoning midseason leaf removal and east-side exposure, shifting to late defoliation, kaolin clay sprays, and canopy-focused vineyard design.
  • At Chêne Bleu in Ventoux, a 40°C heat wave in August 2023 caused significant damage; white varieties now receive no summer leaf removal and Syrah only limited east-side stripping.
  • Tyrrell's in Hunter Valley reports kaolin clay reduces canopy temperature by up to 9°C; sorting sunburned fruit remains the final defense when prevention fails.
