Seven restaurant sommeliers reveal wine markup formulas, 60-76 percent of bill

  • Seven beverage directors from US restaurants shared how they calculate wine markups, which range from 60 to 76 percent of the final menu price across their venues.
  • Kinga Mackowiak at Apple Blossom in Denver said 60-70 percent of a bottle's menu price is markup, leaving the restaurant 38-48 percent profit after accounting for glassware, labor, and credit card fees.
  • William Jones at Deep Roots Restaurant Group in Alabama reported 76 percent average markup but said tariffs, labor costs, and economic pressures have forced tighter margins to meet profit targets.
  • Nayda Freire at Renzo in Charleston uses tiered markups, charging 2.5-4 times the wholesale price depending on initial cost, with glassware and rising insurance expenses driving pricing decisions.
  • Most beverage directors price wine dynamically rather than applying a fixed multiplier, weighing factors like demand, inventory age, exclusivity, and seasonality alongside operational overhead.

Early harvests shift wine tourism and strain production beyond the cellar

  • Grape harvests across France, Germany, California, and Czech Republic arrived six to eight weeks or more ahead of historical norms this summer, with Domaine Lafage in Roussillon picking July 23 for the first time in winemaker Jean-Marc Lafage's career.
  • Aaron Jackson, winemaker at Aaron Wines in Paso Robles, California, reports July visitation to his tasting room drops sharply as heat rises, and direct-to-consumer shipping windows shrink as safe temperature ranges narrow year on year.
  • Šárka Betke, co-founder of the Lacery vineyard in Moravia, Czech Republic, reports wine tourism and shipping were severely limited by summer heat waves exceeding 40 degrees Celsius, hurting cash flow for established regional producers relying on summer sales.
  • Winemakers face rising costs from earlier-peak electricity demand, refrigeration strain, water fees, insurance, and packaging delays when heat prevents label adhesion, plus supply-chain pressure as harvest timing compresses crews and bottling facilities.
  • Wine tourism to regions like Champagne and Burgundy may grow as fall weather improves under climate change, and earlier harvests could align with peak family travel season, offsetting some revenue losses in other channels.

Federal THC limit threatens US hemp beverage retailers' fast-growing market

  • US hemp-infused beverage retail sales rose 135 percent in the 52 weeks to April 2026, reaching $239 million, with potential market valued at $9.9 to $14.9 billion.
  • Art Nergaryan, owner of Art's Specialties wine and cheese shops in Massachusetts, saw business jump 25 to 35 percent after stocking THC drinks, then collapse when state regulators banned their sale in wine stores in May 2024.

  • Volcanic soils from ancient and active eruptions create high-acidity, mineral-driven wines with savory rather than fruity character across multiple continents.

Pierce's Disease Threatens 41% of European vineyards as climate warms

  • Xylella fastidiosa bacterium, which causes Pierce's disease by blocking water transport in vines, has spread to grapevines in Spain, Portugal, Italy, and France since 2016, killing infected plants within years.
  • New climate modelling shows 41.2 percent of European vineyards now face climate risk from Pierce's disease, up from 21.8 percent in earlier estimates, as warming winters allow the pathogen to survive.
  • Vineyard microclimates in river basins and valleys are especially vulnerable because their topography connects otherwise isolated areas where the bacterium can establish hotspots.
  • California growers have learned that early detection, vector management, and sustained research slow spread once the disease is endemic; eradication alone is not feasible at scale.
  • European countries lack coordinated reporting and communication strategies, risking concealment of infections by growers who fear mandatory vine destruction.

04Maker's Table·Meg Maker·1 SEP 2026

M is for Method

  • Traditional method sparkling wines undergo second fermentation in bottle with sugar and yeast, then are disgorged and topped with dosage; wines with no added sugar are called brut nature.
  • Ancestral method sparkling wines complete fermentation in bottle without disgorgement, resulting in fully dry, lightly cloudy, savory wines favored by natural winemakers.
  • Tank-method sparkling wines ferment in pressurized tanks with added sugar and yeast, producing clean, direct styles with limited lees contact.
  • Skin-contact white wines macerate with skins, seeds, and pulp during fermentation, creating color, tannin, and flavor; the tradition originated in the Caucasus.
  • Botrytized wines result from Botrytis cinerea fungus infection, which concentrates sugars and creates honeyed, lightly savory flavors in white grapes.
