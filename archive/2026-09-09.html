Vineyard idling gains traction as producers manage market slowdown

  • A 2025 study of 19 northern Willamette Valley vineyards found idling reduced management costs by an average of 38 percent per acre, with some cutting costs in half.
  • Vineyard idling, a temporary production pause lasting one to three years, allows growers to avoid removal while preserving assets until market conditions improve.
  • Successful idling requires careful management: aggressive pruning, minimal irrigation year-round starting mid-June, weed control, and light fungicide spraying to prevent vineyard damage during dormancy.

Seven volcanic regions beyond Mount Etna produce distinctive mineral wines

  • Volcanic soils from ancient and active eruptions create high-acidity, saline, savory wines across Santorini, Soave, the Canary Islands, Hungary's Tokaji region, Oregon's Willamette Valley, Auvergne, and Lake County California.
  • Volcanic terrain shapes viticulture: Santorini trains vines in low basket shapes to protect from wind; Lanzarote hand-digs individual "hoyos" holes with rock walls due to intense heat and winds.
  • Volcanic soils resist pests and disease, enabling organic farming and preserving old indigenous varieties; the Canary Islands' isolation from phylloxera allowed centuries-old Listán Blanco and Listán Negro vines to survive.

06Maker's Table·Meg Maker·1 SEP 2026

M is for Method

  • Traditional method sparkling wine undergoes second fermentation in bottle with sugar and yeast, then is disgorged and dosed; wines with zero dosage are called brut nature.
  • Ancestral method sparkling wine completes fermentation in bottle without disgorgement, remaining fully dry and cloudy with retained lees; it predates traditional method.
  • Tank method (Charmat/Martinotti) uses pressurized tanks for second fermentation, producing cleaner, fresher sparkling wines with less lees contact than bottle methods.
