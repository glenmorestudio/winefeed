Federal THC cap crushes hemp beverage retailers in regulatory reversal

  • Massachusetts wine and spirits retailer Art Nergaryan saw sales jump 25 to 35 percent after stocking THC drinks, then lost the category entirely when state regulators banned cannabis beverages from liquor stores in May 2024.
  • US hemp-beverage retail sales surged 135 percent to $239 million in the 52 weeks prior to April 2026, but now face collapse from federal and state enforcement.
  • Congress passed legislation in November 2025 capping THC at 0.4 milligrams per container, effective November 12, 2026, which effectively bans most hemp-derived THC beverages currently on sale.
  • The FDA has held since December 2018 that hemp-beverage products are unlawful in interstate commerce, yet states like Minnesota, Georgia and Tennessee created their own legal frameworks, leaving the industry in legal limbo.
  • Retailers, distributors and manufacturers lack federal guidance on which THC products are lawful, forcing many to operate in a legal gray area or exit the market entirely.

Soju surges past shōchū in U.S. market, driven by Korean culture and flavor trends

  • Soju grew 133 percent between 2019 and 2025, while shōchū rose only 11 percent over the same period, according to IWSR consulting director Koryn Ternes for the Americas.
  • Soju remains under one percent of total U.S. spirits volume as of 2025, but demand from younger consumers, Korean cultural interest, and lower alcohol content are driving retail expansion.
  • Green bottle soju, the mass-produced style dominant since a 1965 rice ban in Korea, became the category's primary U.S. export and now dominates retailer purchasing decisions.
  • Craft soju producers like Tokki Soju, founded in Brooklyn in 2016, face consumer loyalty to established green bottle brands like Jinro, though education and maturation may fuel future demand.
  • Shōchū brands including iichiko and Mizu focus on bar placement and higher-proof expressions to compete with craft spirits; bartenders serve as key ambassadors for category awareness outside Japan.

Ancient vine-training systems vanish as labor costs and mechanization reshape European winegrowing

  • Italy's alberata system, once widespread around Naples, survives on only a handful of hectares after being replaced by mechanizable methods like Guyot and vertical shoot positioning.
  • Lanzarote's hoyo pits, carved after 1730s volcanic eruptions, boosted island tourism and wine sales; but labor demands are unsustainable, active vineyards shrank 19 percent between 2000 and 2026 despite recent subsidies.
  • Familia Torres uses machinery adapted to pergola training for Rías Baixas Albariño in Spain's north, reducing labor intensity; mandatory hand-harvesting rules in the DO mean all systems require equal picking effort.
  • Bellussera, a 19th-century pergola hybrid in Veneto that supported silk and subsistence farming, nearly vanished as those industries died; climate instability and wine tourism now offer revival potential.
  • Historic vine-training survival depends on regional economics: hoyo and bellussera systems gain traction where tourism or climate adaptation add value, while labor-intensive methods without local draw continue to fade.

Pierce's Disease Threat Expands Across European Vineyards as Climate Warms

  • Xylella fastidiosa bacteria, which causes Pierce's disease in grapevines, has been detected in Portugal, Spain, Italy, and France since 2013, with a single infected vine found in continental Europe in 2023.
  • A 2025 study using high-resolution climate data found 41.2 percent of European vineyards now face climate risk from Pierce's disease, up from 21.8 percent in previous models, driven by warming winters.
  • Vineyard microclimates near river basins and valleys are especially vulnerable because topographic features connect otherwise isolated areas, creating potential hotspots for disease spread.
  • California growers have managed Pierce's disease for decades through early detection, vector control, and integrated strategies; Europe is still developing coordinated surveillance and communication systems among regions.
  • European Union conducts annual surveys to monitor spread, with estimated potential economic damage at €5.5 billion if the pathogen becomes endemic across the continent.

Disease-resistant hybrid grapes cut fungicide sprays 80 percent across Europe

  • PIWIs, fungus-resistant hybrid grapes bred from American and European vines, require two to three fungicide sprays annually versus eight to 15 for traditional varieties.
  • Genetic testing technology rhAmpSeq, deployed since 2019 via US initiative VitisGen, lets researchers identify and locate resistance genes with precision, enabling faster breeding.
  • Stacking multiple resistance genes protects individual traits from pathogen adaptation, creating stronger and more durable mildew resistance in new varieties.
  • Germany leads PIWI plantings at 4,000 hectares in 2025, four percent of total vineyard area; a 2026 Luxembourg study showed PIWIs delivered twofold economic savings and threefold environmental gains.
  • Bordeaux breeders are developing ResDur varieties crossed with Cabernet Franc and Petit Verdot to retain regional character while reducing chemical inputs.

Vineyards deploy shade netting to cool grape clusters during heat events

  • Shade netting is being repurposed from crop protection into a climate-adaptation tool to lower berry temperature, preserve color compounds, and slow ripening during heat spikes.
  • UC Davis research on Cabernet Sauvignon showed shaded clusters retained 27 percent more anthocyanins than unshaded fruit when anthocyanins degrade above 35 degrees Celsius.
  • At Catena Zapata in Mendoza, Luis Reginato reports vines under light netting (15 percent shade) show less stress in hot conditions; the estate is comparing 17, 50, and zero percent shade nets across multiple seasons.
  • Marco Simonit's 2025 trials at Petra vineyard in Tuscany found Merlot clusters under 90 percent shade nets cooled from 50 to 40-41 degrees Celsius, reducing sunburn from 41 percent to three percent; white and green nets performed better than black.
  • Boroli estate in Barolo discovered that net placement and density matter critically: one-sided positioning prevents heat trapping, and contact between heated fabric and grapes causes burn, requiring careful spacing and material selection.
