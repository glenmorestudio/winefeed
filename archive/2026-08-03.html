US wine industry lacks unified marketing infrastructure to counter health narratives

  • Gino Colangelo, founder of New York PR agency Colangelo & Partners, says US wine has no equivalent to dairy or almond sector marketing bodies that sustain consistent campaigns.
  • Come Over October, a pro-bono campaign Colangelo launched four years ago with wine critic Karen McNeil and PR figure Kimberly Charles, reached 3 billion media impressions but receives inconsistent company funding.
  • Wine regions investing steadily, such as Wines of Georgia, Chianti Classico, Prosecco DOC Consortium, and Chile, build market share through incremental campaigns; sporadic efforts fail to sustain traction.
  • Colangelo argues the current US wine downturn is cyclical, but its depth depends on sustained industry marketing investment; without it, market share erodes rapidly.
  • Competition from THC beverages, GLP-1 weight-loss drugs, and sober-curious trends among younger consumers compound pressure on the industry to fund collective advocacy.

Endeavour Group sells Chapel Hill and most Australian wineries

  • Endeavour Group, the wine division of Australian supermarket chain Woolworths, is selling physical assets of Chapel Hill Estate in McLaren Vale and most other wineries it owns, announced 30 July.
  • The company will retain Chapel Hill, Riddoch and Krondorf brands but sell their vineyards and cellars; only Cape Mentelle and Dorrien Estate in Australia plus Isobel Estates in New Zealand remain fully owned.
  • New CEO Jayne Hrdlicka is pivoting to a model where Endeavour sources 99% of wine from bulk suppliers instead of growing grapes, as part of a AU$300 million cost-cutting restructure.
  • Chapel Hill, acquired for an undisclosed sum in 2019, comprises 13.69 hectares of vineyard, a heritage 1865 chapel, and full winery infrastructure; the listing signals it need not remain a winery.
  • The sale reflects Australia's wine crisis: exports fell to a 22-year low and the 2026 harvest dropped 19% to 1.27 million tonnes, the lowest in 25 years.

Australian wine exports hit 22-year low amid global consumption decline

  • Wine Australia reported exports fell 7% in value to A$2.3bn and 6% in volume to 598 million litres in the year to June 2026, the lowest volume since 2004.
  • Global wine consumption has dropped to its lowest level since 1961, driven by cost-of-living pressures, changing consumer habits and competition from alternative alcoholic beverages.
  • Australia's three largest markets all declined: mainland China fell 15% to A$756m; UK and US shipments reached 25-year lows, with weak demand across most price categories.
  • Canada bucked the trend, rising 20% in value to A$188m, partly from market-share gains during US trade disputes; several Asian markets also grew, with Thailand recording record export values.
  • Peter Bailey, Wine Australia manager of market insights, stated the global decline reflects lasting shifts in consumer behaviour rather than temporary downturn.

Bordeaux wildfires force evacuations, threaten tourism as climate stress mounts

  • Wildfires near Bordeaux forced 220,000 evacuations across France and destroyed around 240 homes; the French government urged tourists to avoid the region at the height of summer.
  • Wine tourism, described by winemaker Edouard Le Grix de la Salle as Bordeaux's only growing business sector, halted abruptly; Château Picque Caillou's general director Peggy Plouhinec said hospitality would suffer from lower tourist numbers.
  • Most vines escaped direct fire damage, but months of intense heat and drought have left vegetation tinder-dry; Thomas Duroux, director general of Château Palmer, warned that water stress could force vines to shut down and shrivel grapes.
  • Smoke taint risk remains contested: consultant Robert Joseph flagged vineyards 50-100 km downwind of major fires as at risk, but db correspondent Colin Hay noted the nearest vineyards sit 15 km away and 2022 fires produced taint only in isolated parcels.
  • Duroux acknowledged climate change has ripened grapes in hot years like 2020, 2022 and 2025, but said Bordeaux now faces an existential crisis requiring water security and vineyard adaptation to withstand future climatic stress.

Ancient vine-training systems vanish as labor costs and mechanization reshape wine regions

  • Italy's alberata method, once widespread around Naples, now covers only a handful of hectares, replaced by mechanizable systems like Guyot as labor costs rose through the late 20th century.
  • Lanzarote's hoyo system, carved into volcanic rock after 1730s eruptions, survives because it drives tourism; active vineyard area fell 19 percent between 2000 and 2026 despite government subsidies.
  • Familia Torres maintains pergola training for Rías Baixas Albariño by adopting specialized machinery for maintenance tasks, though hand-harvesting remains mandatory across the denomination anyway.
  • Venice's 19th-century bellussera hybrid, which once supported silk production and livestock, collapsed when those industries vanished; climate change and wine tourism now offer potential economic revival.
  • Survival of heritage systems depends on local geography, labor availability, and tourism value; no single approach works across regions, but each preservation contributes to viticultural diversity.

Vineyard netting emerges as climate tool to cool fruit zones during heat stress

  • Growers in California, Argentina, Italy, and Piedmont are using shade netting not to block birds or hail, but to reduce cluster-zone temperatures and slow ripening during heat events.
  • UC Davis research on Cabernet Sauvignon found shaded clusters had 27 percent more anthocyanins than unshaded fruit when temperatures exceeded 35°C, the degradation threshold for red wine color.
  • At Petra in Tuscany, Merlot shaded with nets reached 40-41°C versus 50°C in unshaded controls, cutting sunburn incidence from 40-41 percent to as low as three percent under 90 percent shade.
  • Net color matters: white and green nets absorbed less heat than black nets, and placement is critical; fabric touching clusters can itself burn grapes or trap heat and create oven-like microclimates.
  • The strategy requires calibration across canopy management, water use, and site selection; more shade does not automatically mean cooler fruit, and excessive shading risks reducing beneficial UV light and phenolic development.
0213 JUL 2026

Grape sunburn damage spreads as heat waves reach cooler regions

  • Sunburned grapes show flattened fruit, oxidized bitterness, rubber, asphalt, and creosote notes in the finished wine, distinct from normal ripeness or heat-vintage character.
  • Berry surface temperatures can reach 22 to 27 degrees Fahrenheit above air temperature; Chardonnay berries show damage within five minutes at 104 to 109°F under high light.
  • Sunburn browning damages skin color; sunburn necrosis kills cells, cracks skins, and shrivels berries, driven primarily by extreme heat rather than light alone.
  • Producers including Tyrrell's in Hunter Valley and Chêne Bleu in Ventoux have abandoned or severely limited midsummer leaf removal and adopted kaolin clay sprays and canopy management to protect fruit.
  • Earlier defoliation at flowering, not veraison, allows berries time to build photoprotective compounds and reduces sunburn risk compared to late-season exposure.

Mourvèdre producers adapt canopy and irrigation as heat pushes iconic southern French variety

  • Mourvèdre, the heat-loving grape dominant in Bandol rosé and red wines, now struggles to maintain balance and elegance as southern French temperatures rise and drought intensifies.
  • Jean-François Ott at Domaines Ott and other Bandol producers are experimenting with three-dimensional canopy management and higher pruning to shade grapes from extreme heat and prevent sunburn.
  • A three-year drought from 2022 to 2024 killed even old bush vines in Jumilla, Spain, prompting growers and the Jumilla Consejo to conclude irrigation may be essential for vine survival in dry years.
  • Provence viticulturist Alain Deloire warns dry farming cannot sustain Bandol long; Bandol director Olivier Colombano remains cautious but permitted emergency irrigation when vines faced death in 2024.
  • Jumilla's vineyard area has halved in 20 years as southern, hotter sites were abandoned, while producers now favour higher-altitude parcels with deeper soils and stronger water retention.
