London wine merchant Friarwood closes after 59 years in administration

  • Friarwood Fine Wines and Spirits, founded in London in 1967 by Peter Bowen, entered administration on 3 July and closed permanently.
  • Ben Carfagnini, a Canadian-born former intern, took over the business in February 2015 and modernised it with e-commerce, a second store, and expanded ranges beyond Bordeaux and Burgundy.
  • Debts rose from £1.79 million to £2.22 million in the year to March 2025, while assets fell from £675,491 to £319,352.
  • The retail operation, which had grown to two London locations by 2017, contributed roughly one-third of revenue, with the balance from wholesale to hotels, restaurants and private clubs.
  • The business ceased accepting new orders after closure, though existing orders were fulfilled from its Parsons Green store through 7 August.

UK fine wine distributors lose £20m annually amid structural consolidation

  • The top 15 UK fine wine merchants lost nearly £20 million combined in the past year, with £210 million in annual admin costs against £850 million in sales revenue.
  • Jeremy Howard, CEO of Cru World Wine, attributes losses to fragmented distribution chains with duplicated costs, masked during the Covid demand surge but now unsustainable.
  • Industry consolidation is inevitable as smaller merchants cannot afford technology infrastructure and administrative overhead; Helen Miller of Goedhuis Waddesdon cited shared infrastructure as essential to survival.
  • Brett Fleming of Armit Wines expects consolidation to accelerate and intensify across distribution and hospitality, citing inflation, geopolitical tension, and fiscal policy as persistent headwinds.
  • Howard proposes a technology-driven marketplace owned collectively by producers, merchants and collectors to reduce duplicated costs and allow smaller companies to operate with lower overheads.

Ancient vine-training systems face extinction as labor costs and mechanization spread

  • Alberata vines, once common around Naples, now survive on only a handful of hectares, displaced by efficient mechanizable systems like Guyot and vertical shoot positioning.
  • Lanzarote's hoyo pits, dug after 1730s volcanic eruptions to reach soil beneath ash, shrunk from 2,326 hectares in 2000 to 1,889 by 2026 as labor costs and manual work demands pushed growers toward conventional irrigated vineyards.
  • Alberto González Plasencia, owner of Bodegas Timanfaya in Lanzarote, warns that losing hoyos will cut tourism visits and reduce wine value; local subsidies aim to slow the decline.
  • Familia Torres maintains pergola training at Pazo Torre Panelas in Rías Baixas by adapting machinery for ground tasks; hand-harvesting remains mandatory across the region regardless of system.
  • Bellussera, a 19th-century hybrid pergola in Veneto, fell from dominance to nine percent of Piave DOC as the silk industry collapsed and subsistence farming ended; climate resilience and wine tourism may offer revival.

EU quality labels guarantee sparkling wine origin and production standards

  • EU Quality Labels (PDO, PGI, Organic) certify that sparkling wines meet strict standards for origin, production methods, safety, and sustainability across member states.
  • PDO sparkling wines like French Champagne, Italian Prosecco, and Spanish Cava require all production steps within a designated region and use traditional methods with mandatory aging.
  • PGI wines allow more flexibility than PDO: grapes sourced from broader regions and diverse winemaking techniques, enabling producers like Tuscan wineries to make premium styles outside strict provincial rules.
  • Austrian Kamptal PDO sparkling wines, made by small family-owned biodynamic wineries, showcase regional terroir through traditional method production with extended lees aging and crisp acidity.
  • US importers cannot display the EU Organic Label on bottles due to lack of USDA recognition, though organic grapes grown under European standards may still be noted by producers.

Bordeaux launches chilled, low-tannin Claret to attract younger drinkers

  • Syndicat des AOC Bordeaux et Bordeaux Supérieur created Bordeaux Claret, a regulated style of light, fruit-forward red for the 2025 vintage, targeting younger consumers and reversing falling red wine consumption.
  • Wines must meet strict sensory standards: ruby to light-purple colour, short two-to-four day maceration, no oak, 11-13% ABV, served chilled at 5°C, with up to 7g/l residual sugar for the chillable expression.
  • About 40 producers including major brands Calvet and Mouton Cadet made roughly two million bottles in the first vintage, exceeding expectations and signalling serious industry backing.
  • The union recommends labelling as 'Bordeaux Claret' or 'chillable red', with marketing targeted at on-trade venues in France, Belgium, Germany and the UK to build awareness through by-the-glass service.
  • UK merchants resist losing the 'claret' name for traditional Bordeaux, prompting the union to emphasise the broader 'chillable red' descriptor in UK messaging rather than compete for the historic term.

WHO warns alcohol is carcinogen; NHS advises moderation instead

  • WHO classifies alcohol as a Group 1 carcinogen alongside asbestos and tobacco, citing no safe consumption level.
  • UK NHS recommends a more moderate threshold: 14 units weekly for both sexes, roughly 10 small glasses of lower-strength wine.
  • A 175ml glass of 12% ABV wine contains up to 158 calories; low-alcohol alternatives often compensate with residual sugar.
  • Laura Catena, winemaker and physician, argues peer-reviewed studies show moderate drinkers have lower all-cause mortality than abstainers.
  • The debate reflects competing framings: wine as toxic substance versus wine as cultural and social cornerstone tied to pleasure and health.

0127 JUL 2026

Vineyards deploy shade netting to cool grape clusters and preserve quality

  • Shade netting, traditionally used to block hail and birds, is now being tested as climate adaptation to lower cluster temperature and preserve color compounds during heat spikes.
  • UC Davis research on Cabernet Sauvignon at Oakville showed shaded clusters had 27 percent more anthocyanins than unshaded controls when temperatures exceeded 35 degrees Celsius, the degradation threshold.
  • Catena Zapata in Mendoza practices filtered light through canopy management rather than full cluster exposure, relying on nets that intercept roughly 15 percent of radiation in their high-altitude vineyards.
  • Petra estate in Tuscany found Merlot clusters under 90 percent shade nets measured 40-41 degrees Celsius versus 50 degrees in controls; white and green nets outperformed black nets by absorbing less heat.
  • Boroli in Barolo discovered nets must be positioned away from fruit to avoid direct contact burn and that open nets reduce peak hours above critical thresholds better than dense shading.
0213 JUL 2026

Grape sunburn spreads beyond hot climates as heat waves arrive earlier

  • Joanna Gambetta, wine scientist at University of Newcastle Australia, identifies sunburn as a stress response when berry surface temperatures exceed 104-109°F under high light, causing chlorophyll and polyphenols to degrade.
  • Sunburned grapes produce sensory defects including flattened fruit, oxidized bitterness, rubber and asphalt notes, detectable in finished wine by winemakers across Spain, California, France and Oregon.
  • Traditional leaf removal during midsummer now increases sunburn risk; defoliation at flowering builds photoprotective compounds in berries, while removal at veraison or before heat waves causes damage.
  • Producers including Tyrrell's in Hunter Valley and Chêne Bleu in Ventoux have abandoned summer defoliation or shifted to kaolin clay sprays and canopy management to keep fruit shaded.
  • Steve Matthiasson and other winemakers emphasize vineyard sorting of damaged fruit is essential, as sunburned grapes degrade wine quality even in small quantities.

Mourvèdre Struggles as Southern France Heat Exceeds Its Tolerance

  • Mourvèdre, traditionally bred for heat in Bandol and Jumilla, now faces temperatures and drought severity beyond its adaptation limits, threatening vine survival and wine quality.
  • Jean-François Ott at Domaines Ott and producers across Bandol are adopting canopy management techniques, higher pruning, and ground cover crops to shade grapes and retain soil moisture.
  • A three-year drought from 2022 to 2024 killed old Mourvèdre vines in Jumilla; Carolina Martínez Origone, general secretary of Jumilla Consejo, says irrigation may now be essential for vine survival.
  • Bandol's cooler parcels, traditionally reserved for rosé, are shifting to red wine as producers abandon the most vulnerable sites; Olivier Colombano, director of the Bandol appellation, monitors climate impacts region-wide.
  • The 2019 vintage produced structured, age-worthy rosés despite extreme heat; the drier 2021 vintage showed juice concentration and lower phenolic ripeness, signalling style changes ahead.

Global alcohol deaths fall 26 percent since 2010, analysis shows

  • Alcohol-attributable deaths fell 26 percent worldwide between 2010 and 2024, according to analysis by the International Alliance for Responsible Drinking using Global Burden of Disease data.
  • Disability-adjusted life years linked to alcohol use fell 23 percent over the same period, while measured alcohol exposure declined 10 percent.
  • The International Alliance for Responsible Drinking attributes the trend to combined effects of changing drinking patterns, healthcare improvements, public health initiatives, and economic development, not a single intervention.
  • The 26 percent reduction exceeds the UN target of 20 percent harm reduction by 2030, though the alliance notes gains vary significantly by country and region.
